## Executive summary

- **The Galactic bar is the leading physical candidate, but it is not yet an explanation.** Omega Cen’s low-pericentre orbit repeatedly enters the bar’s strong-force region, and published simulations show that a rotating bar can give neighboring debris different torques near pericentre. But the reported PM change is large: at 4.8 kpc, 90 km/s is about **4 mas/yr**. The bar must produce a strongly *differential* kick to the distal arm, not merely change the cluster’s orbit.
- **The apparent 1 kpc displacement and 90 km/s difference are hard to reconcile if both persist over hundreds of Myr.** A 90 km/s velocity difference maintained for 300 Myr corresponds to about 28 kpc of relative travel. A viable perturbation must therefore be brief, project mostly along an unobserved direction, or indicate that the measured ridge is not one continuous stream population.
- **The bar is a better match than the other named smooth perturbers.** LMC reflex motion is largely common to this nearby structure; a smooth halo-shape or disc-mass change should alter the orbit coherently; spiral forcing is weaker at the knee’s estimated Galactocentric height; and a giant molecular cloud or Sagittarius encounter could matter only with a sufficiently close, favorably timed passage.
- **The published spectroscopy supports an ω Cen association near the start of the bend, but does not settle the distal knee.** GALAH reported two candidate stars at \(b=33^\circ\) and \(37^\circ\), with \(v_{\rm los}=215\)–219 km/s and [Fe/H] = −1.88 and −1.53. These lie around \(l\approx-49^\circ\) to \(-51^\circ\), before the reported \(l\approx-30^\circ\) to \(-45^\circ\) knee. I found no published S⁵, APOGEE, or Gaia RVS study that independently samples the distal knee.
- **STREAMFINDER could affect the inferred track and membership, but cannot by itself manufacture the Gaia measurements.** Its candidates are selected for compatibility with trial orbit templates. That makes a template-selection or population-joining artifact testable; it does not make the measured proper motions or distances artifacts automatically.

## Detailed findings

### 1. Which mechanism could make a bend this large?

The requested scale is substantial. Using \(v_t=4.74047\,d\,\mu\), a 90 km/s tangential difference at \(d=4.8\) kpc is \(3.95\) mas/yr. A 15° angular offset at that distance corresponds to 1.26 kpc. These are conversions from the values in the question, not independent measurements.

The location also makes the bar relevant. For a representative knee point near \(l=-38^\circ,\ b=36^\circ,\ d=4.8\) kpc and \(R_0=8.1\) kpc, the Galactocentric cylindrical radius is roughly 5.5–6 kpc and the height is about 2.8 kpc. That places the *present-day knee* near the commonly estimated bar corotation radius, while the cluster’s stated pericentre lies well inside it. Portail et al. measured a bar pattern speed of \(39.0\pm3.5\) km s\(^{-1}\) kpc\(^{-1}\) and a corotation radius \(6.1\pm0.5\) kpc in their model; another analysis found \(33.29\pm1.81\) km s\(^{-1}\) kpc\(^{-1}\), implying a corotation range of 6.5–7.5 kpc under its rotation-curve assumptions. These are model-dependent constraints, not a direct measurement of Omega Cen’s resonance state. ([Portail et al. 2017, arXiv:1608.07954](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.07954); [Clarke & Gerhard 2021, arXiv:2107.10875](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.10875))

A circular-orbit corotation radius is not itself proof that this eccentric, retrograde cluster is resonantly trapped. For eccentric orbits, the relevant condition involves radial and azimuthal frequencies as well as bar speed. A 2026 preprint on a *different, long-term question*—whether the bar could have moved Omega Cen from Gaia-Sausage–Enceladus-like phase space—finds that its proposed migration requires a very slow present-day bar, \(\Omega_b\lesssim26\) km s\(^{-1}\) kpc\(^{-1}\). That is below most current estimates. It therefore does not support a claim that Omega Cen is currently in that particular resonance. It does establish that bar-orbit coupling has been studied explicitly for Omega Cen, under assumptions about its origin and the bar’s past evolution. ([Dillamore, Zhang & Belokurov 2026, preprint, arXiv:2606.12516](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12516))

| Mechanism | Order-of-magnitude assessment for this case |
|---|---|
| **Rotating bar** | **Best candidate; potentially tens of km/s per strong pericentre passage, with a larger cumulative or differential effect possible near a suitable resonance.** As a scale estimate, take a bar force fraction \(Q_b\sim0.05\)–0.15 of the total force at \(R\sim2\) kpc and \(V_c\sim200\) km/s. Then \(a_b\sim Q_bV_c^2/R\), or roughly 1–3 km/s per Myr while traversing the inner few kpc. A 10–20 Myr strong-force passage gives a gross impulse of order 10–60 km/s. This is only an order-of-magnitude bound: the *difference* between debris stars’ impulses depends on their positions and passage times relative to bar orientation. Several pericentric passages in 300–600 Myr make a large differential effect conceivable, but the estimate does not demonstrate a 90 km/s kick. Hattori et al. describe exactly this differential-torque mechanism for Ophiuchus; Pearson et al. find that a rotating bar can reproduce Pal 5’s asymmetry in their models. ([Hattori et al. 2016, arXiv:1512.04536](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04536); [Pearson, Price-Whelan & Johnston 2017, arXiv:1703.04627](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.04627)) |
| **Spiral arms** | **Probably secondary.** Spiral forcing can alter orbits, but the estimated knee height is nearly 3 kpc above the plane, where a thin-disc spiral perturbation is reduced. Omega Cen’s disc crossings could still matter. A few to perhaps tens of km/s is a reasonable scale for a favorable repeated interaction; a localized 90 km/s tangential change is not the default expectation. |
| **Giant molecular clouds** | **Possible only through a close encounter.** The impulse approximation gives \(\Delta v\sim2GM/(bV)\). A \(10^7\,M_\odot\) cloud at \(b=0.1\) kpc and \(V=50\) km/s gives about 17 km/s; a \(10^8\,M_\odot\) complex at that impact parameter could approach 170 km/s. Such a close massive encounter is exceptional, and the stream segment would need to pass near the cloud. This is a targeted-encounter hypothesis, not a generic effect. |
| **LMC and inner-Galaxy reflex motion** | **Small differential effect across this nearby stream segment.** For an illustrative \(10^{11}\,M_\odot\) LMC at 50 kpc, the tidal acceleration difference across 5 kpc is \(GM r/D^3\); integrated for 0.5 Gyr it gives about 9 km/s. The reflex motion measured against distant halo stars can be tens of km/s, but much of that is common-mode for the cluster and its nearby debris. The literature’s \(\sim40\) km/s inner-Galaxy reflex result is measured *relative to stars at about 100 kpc*, not as a 40 km/s differential kick across Fimbulthul. ([Chandra et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.01676](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01676)) |
| **Sagittarius** | **Could be important if a close encounter occurred; otherwise likely subdominant.** As a deliberately favorable example, a \(4\times10^{10}\,M_\odot\) perturber at \(b=20\) kpc and \(V=200\) km/s gives a total impulse near 86 km/s. Across a 5 kpc stream segment, the differential part is much smaller unless the encounter geometry is especially close. Simulations of GD-1-like streams find that recently stripped stars are generally less affected by Sagittarius than older debris, although some configurations produce strong changes. The result depends on encounter geometry and stripping age. ([Dillamore et al. 2022, arXiv:2205.13547](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13547)) |
| **Halo flattening or triaxiality** | **Can change orbital-plane precession and the global track; less naturally makes a sharp, local PM jump.** The STREAMFINDER DR3 atlas’s preferred halo fit has density flattening \(q_{m,h}=0.75\pm0.03\), conditional on its model and stream sample. That is evidence against assuming a spherical halo, not a direct bound on the stream’s short-term kick. A strongly non-axisymmetric halo could make the orbit more complex, but the mechanism would need to explain why the discrepant behavior appears at the distal arm while the near-cluster arm agrees. ([Ibata et al. 2024, arXiv:2311.17202](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17202)) |
| **Disc mass or smooth-potential errors** | **Could shift the integrated orbit; unlikely to produce the whole local anomaly alone.** Changing the disc/bulge force changes pericentre speed and precession. But a smooth axisymmetric change acts on the cluster and recently released debris together. The supplied fact that McMillan17 and DB98 models both miss the knee weakens a simple axisymmetric-potential explanation. It does not test a rotating bar or a live, time-dependent Galaxy. |
| **Omega Cen’s present-day phase-space uncertainty** | **Unlikely to explain 90 km/s if Gaia cluster astrometry and the systemic radial velocity are used.** A 1 mas/yr proper-motion shift at 5.6 kpc is about 27 km/s, so a 90 km/s shift would require roughly 3.4 mas/yr in the cluster’s input PM. That is far larger than the formal uncertainty of a well-measured cluster mean. Distance uncertainty changes the conversion and orbit, but would have to be implausibly large to supply the full mismatch. A full covariance propagation remains important because a small present-day error can accumulate into an appreciable positional error after several orbits. |

The bar literature supports **bar-driven stream reshaping in principle**, especially for low-pericentre streams. Hattori et al. show that nearby debris stars reach pericentre at slightly different times and thus encounter a rotating bar at different orientations; their resulting energy and angular-momentum differences change stream growth. The work focuses on Ophiuchus, not Omega Cen. Pearson et al. show a bar model that can reproduce Pal 5’s leading/trailing length asymmetry and gaps. This is evidence that the mechanism can produce observable stream structure, not evidence that it produces this particular Fimbulthul PM knee. ([Hattori et al. 2016, arXiv:1512.04536](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04536); [Pearson et al. 2017, arXiv:1703.04627](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.04627))

There is also a useful caution from newer work: simulations of the bar’s effect on old substructure find strong dispersion for some low-energy, low-inclination populations, while high-inclination streams can be less affected. That work identifies Pal 5 and Ophiuchus among streams in its bar-susceptible region; it does **not** establish that Fimbulthul is similarly affected. The result depends on the adopted bar model and on the orbit integrals used to classify structures. ([Dillamore et al. 2025, MNRAS, DOI:10.1093/mnras/staf1264](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/staf1264))

**The displacement/velocity tension is the strongest physical objection to a simple bar-kick story.** If a 90 km/s difference had persisted for 300 Myr, it would produce roughly 28 kpc of separation. So a bar explanation needs a fairly specific history: a short impulse near a recent passage, a velocity difference largely along the line of sight or stream direction rather than the sky offset, or a resonant torque that changes the present velocity while leaving a relatively modest projected separation. A forward model must demonstrate all of those together.

### 2. Literature on the bar and Omega Cen or inner-orbit streams

The strongest directly relevant studies are:

- **Ophiuchus:** Hattori, Erkal & Sanders explain how a rotating bar can torque stream stars differently at pericentre and create unusually short or altered streams. This is peer-reviewed MNRAS work, published in 2016; it is a close dynamical analogy, not an Omega Cen-tail model. ([arXiv:1512.04536](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04536); [MNRAS DOI:10.1093/mnras/stw1006](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw1006))
- **Pal 5:** Pearson, Price-Whelan & Johnston report that a rotating-bar model can reproduce the observed length asymmetry and gaps in Pal 5. The paper appeared in *Nature Astronomy* in 2017. Its orbit and data differ from Fimbulthul’s. ([arXiv:1703.04627](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.04627))
- **Pal 5 perturbation evidence:** Erkal, Koposov & Belokurov find Pal 5 density structure inconsistent with an unperturbed stream in their simulations and discuss bar and GMC explanations alongside subhaloes. Their study does not uniquely attribute the observed features to the bar. ([arXiv:1609.01282](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.01282); [MNRAS DOI:10.1093/mnras/stw2584](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw2584))
- **Omega Cen orbit in a bar:** The 2026 Dillamore et al. preprint tests bar-driven migration over the long-term formation history. Its proposed resonance requires a pattern speed below typical current estimates. That is not a model of Fimbulthul’s last 300–600 Myr, and it argues against casually calling Omega Cen “in resonance” just because the pericentre lies inside the bar’s corotation radius. ([arXiv:2606.12516](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12516))

The reported orbital period of about 90 Myr means 300–600 Myr of debris age covers roughly 3–7 periods, consistent with the “4–6 pericentres” estimate in the question. That creates enough repeated inner passages for a time-dependent perturbation to matter. But there is no cited published barred-potential model showing the observed Fimbulthul PM sequence or a 90 km/s differential kick.

### 3. Spectroscopy and the STREAMFINDER-selection question

**Published spectroscopy beyond \(b=30^\circ\):**

- **Ibata et al. 2019:** The discovery/association paper reports high-resolution spectra for five stream stars, closely grouped in velocity and chemically consistent with Omega Cen. The paper’s [Fe/H] measurements for four usable spectra span about −1.4 to −1.8 dex, with mean uncertainty 0.11 dex. These observations supported the original association, but they predate the distal DR3 extension and do not provide a dense RV map of the newly described knee. ([Ibata et al. 2019, *Nature Astronomy*, DOI:10.1038/s41550-019-0751-x; arXiv:1902.09544](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.09544))
- **GALAH:** Simpson et al. searched the known stream region at \(b>30^\circ\), selecting GALAH fields within 4° of the stream track and applying broad velocity, parallax, and PM cuts. They found two new candidate members: at \((l,b)=(311.06^\circ,37.19^\circ)\), \(v_{\rm rad}=218.5\) km/s and [Fe/H] \(=-1.53\pm0.08\); and \((308.75^\circ,32.86^\circ)\), \(v_{\rm rad}=214.8\) km/s and [Fe/H] \(=-1.88\pm0.07\). The two stars are at \(l\approx-49^\circ\) and \(-51^\circ\), so they test the section leading into the bend, not the reported \(l\approx-30^\circ\) to \(-45^\circ\) distal knee. The authors also stress that GALAH’s footprint was sparse and that the original stream candidates were generally too faint for GALAH. ([Simpson et al. 2020, MNRAS 491, 3374, DOI:10.1093/mnras/stz3105; arXiv:1911.01548](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01548))
- **Omega Cen’s inner tidal extensions:** A 2026 VLT/FLAMES study identifies five high-probability members in tidal extensions out to 3.2° from the cluster and finds kinematic and metallicity continuity with the cluster. It is valuable support for ongoing stripping, but those fields do not reach the distant \(b>30^\circ\) knee. ([Kuzma et al. 2026, MNRAS 550, stag1147, DOI:10.1093/mnras/stag1147; arXiv:2605.23474](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23474))

I found no published S⁵, APOGEE, or Gaia RVS member analysis that samples the **distal knee** itself. That is not proof that no individual survey spectra exist there; it means the papers located do not establish the knee’s RV and chemical sequence independently. GALAH is the clearest published independent check along the high-latitude arm, and its two high-latitude candidates are at the pre-knee longitude.

**What STREAMFINDER can and cannot explain:** STREAMFINDER assigns significance to stars as members of groups along trial orbits, using positional and kinematic compatibility. A template-based selection can favor some parts of phase space, miss a complex true stream, or make a catalogued ridge look more continuous than a blind selection would. The original algorithm paper describes orbit-based selection and also warns that structures on complex orbits can be missed. ([Malhan & Ibata 2018, MNRAS 477, 4063, DOI:10.1093/mnras/sty912; arXiv:1804.11338](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.11338))

That does **not** mean the algorithm can invent the measured PMs of selected Gaia stars. The discriminating test is to inspect the underlying Gaia DR3 sources and their local phase-space distribution with no STREAMFINDER membership score or orbit prior: are there two distinct PM/RV clumps that the selected catalog stitches together, or is there a continuous sequence with a sharp but real bend? A blind chemical and spectroscopic check is especially important because the distal tail is crowded and contaminated.

The question’s Gaia XP sample—bright giants at the knee, near the cluster, and in stream 55—has modest sample sizes (16–19 per comparison group). The reported KS \(p=0.17\)–0.80 does not reject a common [M/H] distribution, but it does **not** establish equivalence or prove common origin. The 2026 preprint by Zheng et al. uses Gaia XP synthetic photometry and reports that its scaled simulation does not fully reproduce the observed stream morphology; it is not an independent RV test of the knee. ([Zheng et al. 2026, preprint, arXiv:2603.02904](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02904))

### 4. Ranked decisive, cheap tests

1. **Get independent RVs for a small, clean sample of knee giants.** Start with Gaia XP bright giants and cross-match GALAH, Gaia RVS, APOGEE, and any existing southern follow-up spectra. A dozen or more secure giants across both the pre-knee arm and the \(l=-30^\circ\) to \(-45^\circ\) knee would quickly test whether \(v_{\rm los}\) is continuous and whether the knee is one population. The published GALAH candidates at \(l\sim-50^\circ,\ b>30^\circ\) provide a useful anchor, but do not cover the disputed segment.
2. **Make a blind Gaia phase-space plot from the underlying sources.** Reproduce the PM–track and distance trends without STREAMFINDER membership weights, then compare the candidate ridge with local field-star controls. Look for bimodality, gaps, scan-law structure, and sensitivity to the membership threshold. This directly tests template joining at low cost.
3. **Forward-model the bar, with the stream and observations treated together.** Add a rotating bar over a grid of plausible pattern speeds, bar strengths, orientations, and slowdown histories to the validated cluster model; include the actual release-time distribution. Score sky track, distance, both PM components, RV, and counts jointly. A successful model must reproduce the velocity swing *and* the modest positional offset; matching only the sky curve is not enough.
4. **Re-run backward tests with a time-dependent barred potential and full cluster covariance.** The supplied axisymmetric backtracking result makes this useful. Compare return distances and velocities for knee stars and the \(b<30^\circ\) controls, marginalizing over distance, PM, RV, and bar parameters.
5. **Check close-encounter alternatives only after reconstructing the orbit through the disc.** Use the reconstructed stream crossing locations to search for plausible GMC or Sagittarius encounters. These are less compelling without a geometrically close passage, but the impulse estimates show why a specific close encounter should not be dismissed out of hand.

**The strongest argument against the bar as the answer:** the reported proper-motion difference is so large that, if it represented a persistent velocity offset in one cohort, it should create far more than a 1 kpc displacement over the stated debris age. The bar literature shows that it can restructure streams, but not that a modern \(35\)–\(45\) km s\(^{-1}\) kpc\(^{-1}\) bar produces this particular local combination of PM, track, distance, and RV. The bar is the first mechanism to test; the data do not yet justify calling it the cause.

## Disagreements, uncertainties and open questions

- The supplied distance, XP, and radial-velocity results favor continuity: the distance declines smoothly rather than stepping, knee giants have an Omega-Cen-compatible metallicity distribution, and \(v_{\rm los}\) is continuous. Those are meaningful checks, but the chemical samples are small and the distal knee lacks published dense spectroscopy.
- The bar pattern speed is unsettled. Published estimates span roughly 33–40 km s\(^{-1}\) kpc\(^{-1}\) for slow-bar models, while other methods admit faster values. Corotation estimates depend on the adopted rotation curve. The orbit’s being inside or near corotation at some radii does not prove resonant trapping.
- The bar’s differential effect depends on its orientation at each pericentre, bar growth or slowdown, and the debris’ release phases. A static bar model alone may not capture the history.
- The halo flattening result from the STREAMFINDER atlas is conditional on that paper’s stream sample and potential family. It does not decide whether an unmodeled triaxial halo can affect this orbit.
- Survey selection and classification remain unresolved. A continuous CMD-distance and velocity trend is evidence against a simple unrelated foreground group, but does not by itself rule out two kinematically distinct populations selected into one apparent track.
- The 2026 Omega Cen bar-migration result addresses the cluster’s ancient origin, not the present stream’s last few hundred Myr. It should not be treated as evidence that the observed knee is bar-induced.
